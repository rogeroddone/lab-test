Challenges

Uber began six years ago as a way for 100 friends in San Francisco to get luxury rides—everyone’s private driver. Today, we’re a transportation network spanning 450 cities in 72 countries. That network delivers food and packages, as well as people, all at the push of a button. It became clear that a limited set of visual identity components was not enough to engage with different audiences and price points.

BEFORE

— Moving people
— Serving a handful of markets

After

— A range of economy and luxury options
— Moving people, food, and packages
— Serving 72 countries and 450 cities

Previous visuaL identity

Uber’s previous visual identity was too rigid and limitedIt didn’t allow us to express the nuances necessary to communicate with different audiences and price points.

Concept

With a push of a button, Uber offers reliable and affordable rides, as well as food and delivery services. Our technology (Bits) helps move the physical world (Atoms).

Uber exists in the place where Bits & Atoms come together. That is Uber. Not just technology, but technology that moves cities. That’s the story of Bits & Atoms.

Bits & Atoms is the core idea that inspired our new visual identity

Visual identity components

Our visual identity caters to our complex needs as a brand at every touch-point. It’s made up of primary and secondary design components.Connecting Bits & Atoms to create a dynamic visual identity system

Primary components

These are the foundation of our visual identity. They lean close to the Bit, our logical side, and create consistency across our brand visual expressions.

— Bits - high tech
— Monochromatic
— Consistent

We looked at thousands of existing typefaces for inspiration for our custom logotype and identified two options that had potential to serve as a starting point:

Breuer Text Bold - confident
We felt this geometric and precise typeface had the right basic structure to represent our confident and high-tech side.

FS Joey Bold - approachable
We felt this organic, rounded typeface would reflect our approachability without losing our premium feel.

Black & white

Since the bit is binary, we represent technology with two colors: black & white. These colors act as grounding elements, both reflecting Uber’s past and providing consistency within our variable visual identity.

Grid

The grid represents our logical side. It’s the foundation for organizing all the design components. You won’t see it, but it’s always there, keeping the different components of the visual identity clear, cohesive, and beautiful. Like our technology, it’s invisible.

Typography

Clan Pro for Uber is used to reinforce our identity and guarantee excellent legibility in all media. We customized Clan Pro to make it unique to Uber. Most of its personality was preserved, but we made modifications to increase legibility in body copy. For example, we elongated ascenders and descen- ders and increased the size of counters.

Secondary components

These provide flexibility and allow for localization, keeping the system fresh and dynamic. Secondary components lean closer to the Atom, representing the physical world.

— Atoms
- physical world
— Colorful, approachable
— Variable

COLOR

Our color spectrum allows unique combinations that can speak to specific locations, holidays, and partnerships Inspired by the colors of the physical world, we created a broad color spectrum: 22 different colors and 22 values, 484 unique hues in total.

Patterns

Unique patterns are used to add flavor and flexibility. Like colors, they can speak to specific locations, holidays, partnerships, or global themes.More than 70 unique patterns were created­—one for each of the countries we serve

Photography

We use photos to create local stories about the daily lives of our riders and drivers. Our photographs use natural light and capture unposed moments.

Illustrations

Grabbing elements from Art Deco and inspiration from Bits & Atoms, we created something uniquely ours.

COLOR SYSTEM

A variety of global color sets, along with the local themes were created in a systematic way to allow the brand to be expressive while consistent.

Uber
Mobility platform
2016 Global Rebrand

Back in 2014, when I joined Uber, the company had outgrown its identity. What began as a premium ride service had become, during a period of hyper-growth, a global platform spanning dozens of products and price points. The existing brand had no language for that complexity.

Working closely with the head of design and Uber's leadership, the answer was a core concept called Bits & Atoms: technology meeting the physical world. That tension became the foundation for the entire design framework, structured in two parts: consistent monochromatic components rooted in the Bit, creating coherence across every expression; and colorful, variable components rooted in the Atom, enabling localization and keeping the system fresh across markets.


Drove the project end to end. Defined the design framework, crafted the Uber wordmark and its sub-brands, created the color spectrum and established the color architecture. Built and maintained the brand guidelines portal, defining guidelines, templates, and branded examples across print, packaging, web, and product.

Team: Head of Design: Shalin Amin · Design Manager: Strahan McMullen · Lead Brand Designer: Roger Oddone · Designers: Bryant Jow, Catherine Ray, Donald Wong, James Bamford, Lian Ng, Matt Riley, Mirtho Prepont.

2016

Material design

My contribution was twofold: evolving the flat icon style to carry more tactile, paper-like depth while staying true to its geometric foundation, and building Google's color system from scratch. For color, I started with Google's four brand colors and expanded outward, building a full spectrum that designers across the company could share. To make the system usable at scale, I introduced a numerical naming convention, 50 through 900, assigning a value to each tone in the spectrum. That structure became standard practice across the industry after Material Design launched.

As the color spectrum expanded, traditional naming conventions like 'light blue' or 'dark blue' became imprecise and difficult to scale.

To address this, we transitioned to a systematic, numeric approach that provides a logical framework for color classification and consistency.

KENNEDY GUIDELINES

Before Material Design, as part of Project Kennedy, I built Google's first company-wide visual asset guidelines: a comprehensive framework covering product icons, logo lockups, UI icons, and illustration principles. The goal was a document rigorous enough that a vendor in a different timezone could produce work indistinguishable from work made in-house. The guidelines shipped internally in 2012 and were published on Behance in 2013, where they reached over 1 million views and were featured as Best of Behance. See them here: Part 1 and Part 2.

HG26
Particle system /
Textural typeface
Creative codingGenerative design

HG26 is an experimental typeface where the letterforms are vessels, the particles are the texture that fills them, and interaction is what sets them in motion. The grains behave like sand in an hourglass, some drawn toward the cursor and pushed away by it, others left to fall under gravity.


It began as an exploration of particles, and the typeface emerged as the vessel that gave them purpose. As much as anything, it is built to be played with.

Aero
Airline
Brand identity refreshJet livery design

Aero is a semi-private airline offering a premium, intimate flying experience between select destinations.

Aero came to me in 2019 to design the livery for their first plane and revisit the logo. The goal was to elevate the perception of luxury. My advice was to keep the symbol and wordmark but refine the palette, removing the blue tones and developing a warmer spectrum of yellows, oranges, and reds that better expressed the sunrise idea at the core of the brand.

Rather than forcing the window color stripes onto other parts of the plane, we deconstructed the color component of the symbol and morphed it to fit the fuselage. The symbol was placed in the first window, and the staircase was wrapped in the gradient palette, turning boarding into part of the experience.


Testing the livery on black revealed something unexpected: the warm tones against dark backgrounds made the palette richer and more distinctive among airlines. That discovery became the foundation for a broader identity system built around Haas Grotesk and a restrained Swiss-inspired grid, flexing across boarding passes, destination cards, and amenity packaging while keeping the identity unmistakably Aero.

After Uber, I started Oddone, a brand identity studio in San Francisco specializing in visual identities for forward-thinking businesses.

The studio's own identity was the first project, and being our own client is always hard. Partnering with brand strategist Caren Williams was essential in this process of self-discovery, forcing clarity about my values. The core idea that emerged: I explore every angle of an idea. The "O" is treated as a three-dimensional object that reveals a different form depending on the perspective, so that each view shows a different dimension of the same thinking. Strategy, design, and exploration, finally made visible.


After launching, I decided to push the system further, creating a series of expressive posters rooted in the same visual logic, developing a motion language for the brand in the process.

Lab

Hands-on designer. 20+ years defining brand identities and building the systems that carry them.

When I start a branding project, the first thing I ask a client is what makes them different in their category. It's only fair to turn that question on myself.

The answer isn't passion, or being a little obsessed. Every designer says that. It's that more than twenty years in, I'm still the one making the work. I've rebranded a company operating in sixty five countries, built identity systems that hundreds of designers use every day, and worked directly with founders shaping brands from nothing. At every point where the path bent toward managing the work, I found a way to keep making it instead.

I've heard it said that some designers make beautiful work that doesn't mean anything, and some make meaningful work nobody wants to look at. The whole craft is refusing that tradeoff. I start from an idea and let it hold everything else up. When a decision can't be traced back to one, it usually isn't finished.

Along the way I learned that a brand isn't built by one person. It's built by everyone in a company, every day, in a thousand small expressions. So my job is to design the building blocks they reach for, so that what they make comes out considered, efficient, and unmistakably on brand. Staying hands on has meant staying a student of everything that feeds that. Strategy. Motion. Working alongside product designers, learning how a brand puts personality into something otherwise purely functional. Lately, building my own tools and prototypes with AI as my coding partner, so an idea can be tested in a browser the same week it's sketched.

I'm drawn to work that outlives its moment. Swiss and Danish design taught me to strip out whatever isn't earning its place. If something feels gratuitous, there's a reason it's there.
